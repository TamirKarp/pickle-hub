How to pickle and ferment at home, explained properly: the science, tested methods, a brine calculator and hand-picked videos.

Pickling vs. fermenting

Both are old ways of keeping food, and both leave you with something sour and tangy in a jar, which is why people call the results of both "pickles". The difference is where the sourness comes from.

When you pickle, you add the acid yourself: vegetables go into vinegar and are ready within hours. When you ferment, you add salt and let bacteria that already live on the vegetables make the acid for you, turning the food's natural sugars into lactic acid over days or weeks. So not every pickle is fermented, and not every fermented food is a pickle.

What they have in common: both methods make the food acidic enough (below pH 4.6) that harmful bacteria, including the one that causes botulism, can't grow. The science section explains why that number matters.

A note on storage: vinegar pickles can only live in the cupboard if the jars have been properly heat-processed and sealed. Quick "fridge pickles" stay in the fridge. Fermented veg also belongs in the fridge once it tastes right, because the cold slows fermentation and keeps it crunchy.

Sources: Kilner, "What is the difference between fermenting and pickling?"; Wendy the Food Scientist, "Fermentation vs. Pickling".

The Science

Food goes off for two main reasons: microbes (bacteria, yeasts and moulds) grow on it, and the food's own enzymes keep working after it's harvested. Preserving food means changing the conditions so the wrong microbes can't thrive.

There are only a handful of ways to do that: heat, drying, cold, lowering the pH (making it more acidic), cutting off oxygen and taking away the nutrients microbes feed on. Pickling and fermenting lean mostly on acid. Fermenting adds salt and keeps the food under brine, away from the air.

Acid and the pH 4.6 line

pH measures acidity: the lower the number, the more acidic. Below pH 4.6, the bacteria that cause botulism can't grow, and that's the target for both methods. Vinegar gets a pickle there straight away. A ferment gets there gradually, as lactic acid bacteria eat the sugars in the veg and turn them into lactic acid. That acid is also what gives ferments their tang.

Two kinds of fermentation

In lactic acid fermentation, bacteria turn sugars into lactic acid. It's how sauerkraut, kimchi, fermented pickles, yoghurt and some cured meats are made. In alcoholic fermentation, yeasts turn sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide, which is how beer and wine are made. Fruit left to its own devices tends to go the alcoholic route, because yeasts cope with fruit's acidity better than most bacteria do.

Why acid isn't the whole story: yeasts and moulds

Yeasts and moulds are fungi, and unlike most bacteria they're comfortable in acid. Yeasts grow best between about pH 3.5 and 4.5, which is exactly where a finished ferment sits. Moulds tolerate anything from about pH 3.5 to 8. So a jar that's acidic enough to be safe from botulism can still grow a film or mould on top.

Moulds spread through spores that drift through the air and settle on food, and they need oxygen to grow. That's why fermenting recipes are so insistent about keeping everything below the brine with a weight: no air, no mould.

Both also need water. Microbes can only use "free" water, which scientists measure as water activity, on a scale from 0 (bone dry) to 1 (pure water). Yeasts stop growing below about 0.9 and moulds below about 0.8. Very salty or sugary foods, like salt cod or jam, preserve themselves partly this way. Fermenting brines are far weaker than that, so in a ferment the salt's job is to favour the good bacteria, not to stop yeasts and moulds on its own.

Cold

Keeping food below 4°C slows microbes down and slows the chemical reactions that spoil food. It can't undo spoilage that has already started, only delay it. For ferments, moving the jar to the fridge is how you hit the pause button once it tastes the way you like.

Why eat fermented food?

Fermenting doesn't just preserve food. Live ferments carry the bacteria that made them, and there's growing evidence that eating them regularly is good for your gut.

Getting started

You don't need special kit or experience: a clean jar, the right salt, some vegetables and a bit of patience will do. The best first project is a single, simple one, like sauerkraut.

Two ways to ferment veg

Dry salting is for vegetables that give up their own juice, such as cabbage, carrots, beetroot, radishes and onions. You sprinkle salt over them, massage it in and pack them down until they're sitting in their own brine. Brining is for whole or chunky vegetables that won't make enough liquid, such as cucumbers, green beans, cauliflower, peppers and garlic. You pour salty water over them instead.

What you need

  • A jar. Wide-mouthed is easiest to pack. Old jam or pasta sauce jars work fine.
  • A weight to keep everything under the brine. A fermentation weight is ideal, but a small zip-lock bag filled with brine or a shot glass does the job.
  • A lid. A fermentation lid lets gas out by itself. With a normal lid, rest it loosely or open the jar once a day to let the gas out.
  • A plate or tray underneath, because lively ferments can bubble over.
  • Kitchen scales. Salt is the one thing worth weighing precisely.

Salt

Use sea salt, pickling salt or kosher salt. Avoid iodised salt and salt with anti-caking agents, which can hold back the bacteria you want. Always weigh it rather than using spoons: a tablespoon of fine sea salt weighs almost twice as much as a tablespoon of coarse kosher salt.

StrengthUse it for
2% of the veg's weightDry-salted ferments like sauerkraut and kimchi (20 g salt per 1 kg of veg)
3% of the waterA lighter brine for quick or delicate ferments
3.5% of the waterThe all-purpose brine (35 g salt per litre of water)
5% of the waterHot weather, long ferments or extra crunch

Where to keep it

Find a spot out of direct sunlight, away from the oven, radiators and outside walls, where the temperature stays steady and you'll remember to check it every day. Temperature sets the pace:

TemperatureWhat happensTypical time

Keeping things clean

Wash everything (jars, weights, lids, your hands and the worktop) in hot, soapy water, rinse well and leave it to air dry. A dishwasher is fine for cleaning. For vegetable ferments that's enough: the lactic acid the bacteria make protects the jar, so you don't need to sanitise. Do sanitise if your home has damp or mould problems, or if your last batch grew mould or a yeast film. Two things to avoid: don't plunge glass jars into boiling water, because they can crack, and don't clean with vinegar and bicarbonate of soda together, because they cancel each other out.

Is it working?

Start tasting after about five days. Bubbles mean the bacteria are busy, and as the bubbling slows the main fermentation is winding down. A healthy ferment smells pleasantly sour, and cloudy brine and a fizz when you open the jar are both normal. When it tastes right to you, move it to the fridge. Most fermented veg keeps for months there.

Throw it out if you see fuzzy mould, if the veg turns slimy or if it smells rotten rather than sour. The safety section covers what's harmless and what isn't.

Ferment what's in season, and what's left over

Produce fermented at its peak gives the freshest, best-flavoured results, and fermenting is a good way to stop food going to waste. Slightly tired or bruised vegetables are fine once you've trimmed the bad bits. Even scraps can be useful: pineapple peel makes tepache (a fizzy drink), ginger peel can start a ginger bug and watermelon rind can be pickled in brine.

    Technique

    Sauerkraut

    Sauerkraut is just cabbage and salt. The salt draws the water out of the cabbage to make its own brine, and the bacteria already living on the leaves do the rest, turning it tangy over a few weeks.

    Good to know: keep the cabbage pressed under its brine the whole time, because anything poking above it can go mouldy. Weigh your salt (the brine calculator will work it out), taste from about day 5, and move the jar to the fridge once it's as sour as you like.

    Technique

    Brine ferments

    Brine ferments are for whole or chunky veg, and even fruit, that won't make enough juice of their own: you cover them in salty water and let the bacteria get to work.

    Most of the recipes below weigh the veg and water together and add salt as a percentage of the total, which is exactly what the brine calculator does.

    Good to know: keep everything under the brine, using a weight if anything floats, and don't go below 2% salt. Taste from day 2 to 5, then move the jar to the fridge once it's as sour as you like.

    Technique

    Kimchi

    Kimchi is Korea's great fermented side dish: salted napa cabbage coated in a paste of chilli, garlic, ginger and fish sauce, then left to ferment until it's sour, spicy and a little fizzy.

    Unlike sauerkraut, the cabbage is usually salted first and then rinsed, so there's no salt percentage to weigh. The courgette version below is the exception: it's salted at 2% like a kraut.

    Good to know: wear gloves when you mix in the chilli paste, press the kimchi down so it sits under its own juice, and leave room in the jar because it fizzes. A day or two at room temperature, then the fridge, where it keeps getting more sour.
